What Are L-Arginine and L-Citrulline?

Last reviewed: September 2026

If you've read about VapoCept or any circulation-focused supplement, you've probably run into these two names side by side: L-Arginine and L-Citrulline. They're closely related, they're both amino acids, and they both point back to the same underlying process — nitric oxide production. But they aren't interchangeable, and understanding the difference actually matters if you want to know what a formula is doing and why.

What Is L-Arginine?

L-Arginine is a semi-essential amino acid, meaning your body can produce some of it on its own, but dietary intake still matters, especially as demand increases with age or certain health conditions. It's found naturally in foods like poultry, fish, dairy, nuts, and seeds. Its best-known role is as the direct precursor to nitric oxide (NO), a molecule your body produces using an enzyme called nitric oxide synthase.

Nitric oxide is a signaling molecule that helps blood vessels relax and widen — a process called vasodilation. That matters because wider, more relaxed blood vessels allow blood to flow more easily, which is one reason L-Arginine has been studied in the context of circulation, blood pressure, and exercise performance for decades.

Where L-Citrulline Fits In

L-Citrulline is also an amino acid, and it's actually a byproduct of the same nitric oxide reaction that consumes arginine. Here's the interesting part: once citrulline is produced, the kidneys can convert it back into arginine, which then re-enters the nitric oxide pathway. This is often called the "arginine-citrulline cycle," and it's one reason researchers have taken a specific interest in citrulline as a supplement ingredient.

Counterintuitively, several studies have found that oral L-Citrulline supplementation can raise blood arginine levels more efficiently than taking arginine directly. That's because a meaningful portion of orally ingested arginine gets broken down by enzymes in the gut and liver before it ever reaches circulation — a process called first-pass metabolism. Citrulline appears to largely bypass that issue, making it into the bloodstream where the kidneys can then convert it into usable arginine.

Why Formulas Often Combine Both

Because arginine acts more immediately in the nitric oxide pathway, and citrulline provides a more sustained, recycled supply of arginine over time, many formulas — VapoCept included — combine multiple forms of each: for example, L-Arginine, a bonded L-Arginine AKG (alpha-ketoglutarate) extract, L-Citrulline HCL, and L-Citrulline Malate. The idea behind combining several forms is to support both a more immediate effect and a more sustained one across the day, rather than relying on a single spike.

What the Research Generally Shows

Human studies on arginine and citrulline have looked mostly at cardiovascular markers (like blood pressure and vascular function) and exercise performance, with a smaller but growing body of research examining circulation-related cognitive outcomes, since healthy blood flow is one of several factors associated with brain function. It's worth being precise here: this research supports a biological rationale for why these ingredients are included in circulation-focused formulas — it is not the same as a guarantee that any specific product will produce a specific cognitive result for any specific person.

Safety Considerations

Both amino acids are generally recognized as well-tolerated at typical supplemental doses, but they are not risk-free for everyone. People taking blood pressure medication, nitrates, or medications for erectile dysfunction should talk to a doctor before supplementing, since combining nitric-oxide-supporting compounds with those medications can amplify blood-pressure-lowering effects. Pregnant or nursing individuals, and anyone with a diagnosed cardiovascular or kidney condition, should also check with a healthcare provider first.
